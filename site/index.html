Skin cancer, explained calmly

Knowing what we look for takes the fear out of it.

Here is what our doctors are checking for, in plain words, and how each one is usually looked after.

Sunspots

Solar keratoses

Rough, scaly patches on skin that has seen a lot of sun. They are not skin cancer, but they show sun damage and some can change over time.

How they are looked afterOften freezing, a prescription cream, or a cream-and-light treatment called photodynamic therapy.

Basal cell carcinoma

BCC

The most common skin cancer in Australia. It often looks like a pearly or pink bump, or a sore that will not heal, and it usually grows slowly.

How it is looked afterMost often by removing it. Some shallow ones can be treated with creams, light therapy or laser.

Squamous cell carcinoma

SCC

Often a thickened, scaly or tender spot on sun-exposed skin that grows over weeks or months.

How it is looked afterUsually by removing it. Finding it early keeps treatment simpler.

Melanoma

New or changing spots

Can show up as a new spot, or a mole that changes in size, shape or colour. Regular checks and comparing images over time help doctors notice change early.

How it is looked afterTreatment usually starts with removing the spot, and your doctor explains every next step.

General information only. Your doctor will explain what applies to you.

Skin conditions, looked after here

Skin health is more than skin checks.

The everyday conditions that make life uncomfortable are looked after by the same two doctors, in the same calm room.

Acne

At any age

Breakouts that keep coming back, on the face, chest or back, long after you were told you would grow out of them.

How it is looked afterPrescription creams or tablets, and in-clinic treatments where they suit your skin. Your doctor explains how long it usually takes to settle.

Eczema and dermatitis

Itch, dryness, flares

Skin that is itchy, dry or inflamed, flaring for reasons that are hard to pin down.

How it is looked afterFinding what sets it off, including patch testing when that helps, then a plan for flares and for keeping skin calm in between.

Psoriasis

Thickened, scaly patches

Patches that build up on elbows, knees, the scalp or elsewhere, and come and go over the years.

How it is looked afterCreams, light therapy or tablets, chosen with you depending on how much skin is involved.

Rosacea

Redness and flushing

Flushing, redness or visible vessels across the cheeks and nose, often worse with heat, wine or stress.

How it is looked afterYour triggers explained, prescription treatment, and laser where it suits your skin.

Vitiligo

Loss of colour

Patches where the skin loses its colour, which can spread slowly and matter a great deal to how you feel.

How it is looked afterYour doctor explains what is happening and the options, and sun protection is part of the conversation.

Heavy sweating

Hyperhidrosis

Underarm sweating that decides what you wear and how you plan your day.

How it is looked afterTalked through at a consultation, including miraDry, which we have here in the clinic.

Scars and birthmarks

Raised, red or simply there

A scar that stayed raised or red, or a birthmark you would like a doctor to look at properly.

How it is looked afterLooked at in person first. Some are watched over time, some are treated with laser or a small procedure.

Children's skin

Little ones too

Rashes, eczema and birthmarks in children, when you would rather have someone look than keep wondering.

How it is looked afterAn unhurried appointment, explained to you and to them, in words they can follow.

Questions people ask us

The things you were going to look up at 11pm.

Short, honest answers, so you can stop wondering and get it looked at.

When should I worry about a spot?

If it is new, changing in size, shape or colour, itching or bleeding, have it looked at. Most turn out to be nothing, and the ones that matter are simpler to treat when they are found early.

What does melanoma look like?

There is no single look, which is exactly why checks matter. It can be a new spot, or a mole that changes. Your doctor looks at the pattern under a dermatoscope and compares images over time.

How often should I have a skin check?

Most adults have one each year. Your doctor may suggest a different rhythm based on your skin, your family history and how much sun you have had.

Do I need a referral?

Dr Ali can see you with or without a referral from your GP. If you would like to see Dr Colin, call us on (07) 3188 5474 and we will talk you through it.
